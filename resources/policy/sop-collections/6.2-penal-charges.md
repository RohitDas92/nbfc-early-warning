A penalty for non-compliance with a material term of the loan contract is a
**penal charge**. It is not penal interest.

Paragraph 30(1) of the RBI (NBFC – Responsible Business Conduct) Directions, 2025
provides that a penalty, if charged, for non-compliance of material terms and
conditions by the borrower shall be treated as a penal charge and **shall not be
levied in the form of penal interest added to the rate of interest** charged on
the advance, and that there shall be **no capitalisation of penal charges** — no
further interest computed on such charges.

Note (ii) to that paragraph confirms that default in repayment is itself a type
of non-compliance, that any penalty for it may be levied only as a penal charge,
and that it must be reasonable and levied only on the amount under default, in a
non-discriminatory manner under a Board-approved policy. Note (iii) prohibits
levying additional or fresh penal charges on an earlier outstanding amount of
penal charges.

Paragraph 30(1) does permit interest on unpaid interest, including on an unpaid
EMI, at the **contracted** rate until remediation. That is normal compounding and
is not a penal charge. Staff must not conflate the two.

Because an education loan to a student is a loan to an individual for a purpose
other than business, paragraph 30(5) applies: the penal charges must not be
higher than those applicable to non-individual borrowers.

The operational consequence for collections is that the arrears figure quoted to
a borrower, and the amount used to compute any settlement, must separate the
overdue instalments from any penal charges. A quoted figure that silently merges
the two overstates the contractual arrears.

Staff must not describe a penal charge as interest, and must not agree to add one
to the outstanding principal for the borrower's convenience — that would
capitalise it, which paragraph 30(1) forbids. Paragraph 30(6) requires the
quantum and reason for any penal charge to be disclosed, and any instance of levy
to be communicated to the borrower.
